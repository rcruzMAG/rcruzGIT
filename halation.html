How film halation works

When a bright light source hits film, most light is absorbed by the emulsion layers — but some keeps going, passes through the transparent film base, and reflects off the back of the base (and the camera's pressure plate) back up into the emulsion. The reflected light re-exposes the film in a ring around the light source.

The red-sensitive layer sits deepest, closest to the base, so the reflected light mostly re-exposes red — that's why halation glows red-orange, not white. Most stocks fight it with a dark anti-halation backing; CineStill 800T famously has it removed, giving its signature red halos around street lights and neon.

Bloom is different: it's light scattering in the lens and within the emulsion itself, producing a soft, neutral-colored veil around highlights. Real photos on film show both at once — tune them independently below.
